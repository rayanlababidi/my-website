01

What Commissioning Is

Commissioning is the bridge between a machine being physically installed and a machine being allowed to operate. It is a structured, evidence-based process: every system that has to work together (mechanical, electrical, hydraulic, and control) is proven individually and then proven together, in a defined sequence, before the equipment is handed over for service.

The word gets used loosely in casual conversation, but in an industrial setting it has a precise meaning: nothing is assumed to work because it was built correctly. It is demonstrated to work, with recorded evidence, under controlled conditions.

02

Mechanical Completion vs. Commissioning

These two phases are often confused, and the distinction matters. Mechanical completion means the physical installation is finished: piping is connected, equipment is set and aligned, wiring is landed, insulation is installed. It answers the question: is the equipment built as designed?

Commissioning starts where mechanical completion ends. It answers a different question: does the equipment function as designed, under real operating conditions, safely and repeatably? A system can be 100% mechanically complete and still fail commissioning if a sensor is miscalibrated, a valve doesn't stroke through its full range, or a control loop is unstable.

03

System Walkdowns & Readiness Verification

A walkdown is a physical, hands-on inspection of a system before it's energized or put into service. It's deliberately unglamorous: walking every line, checking every valve position, confirming every tag matches the drawing, verifying nothing was left disconnected, capped incorrectly, or installed backward.

The discipline behind a walkdown is that it happens before the system is trusted, not after something goes wrong. A readiness verification builds on this by formally checking off each prerequisite (utilities available, instrumentation calibrated, safety systems armed) against a checklist that someone else could follow and reach the same conclusion.

A walkdown exists to find the problem before the problem finds you.
04

Auxiliary Systems, at a High Level

Large rotating equipment depends on a set of support systems that rarely get public attention but determine whether the main machine can run at all. In general terms:

Lubrication keeps bearings and rotating interfaces from generating destructive friction and heat. Cooling removes heat that the process itself generates, keeping materials within their design temperature limits. Fuel and support systems deliver the energy input the machine needs, cleanly and at the right pressure and flow. Each of these has its own commissioning sequence (filled, vented, pressure-tested, and functionally proven) before it's asked to support the main equipment during startup.

Instrumentation readiness means every sensor the control system relies on has been calibrated and its signal verified end-to-end, from the physical measurement point to the display the operator or control system actually reads.

05

Permissives, Interlocks & First-Rotation Philosophy

A permissive is a condition that must be true before an action is allowed: think of it as a logical gate. Conceptually: "this equipment may not start unless lubrication pressure is adequate, cooling is established, and no safety device is tripped." An interlock is closely related: it's a protective response that stops or blocks an action automatically when a condition becomes unsafe.

The philosophy behind a first rotation (the first time a piece of rotating equipment is actually turned) is to prove the smallest, safest increment first. Slow roll before fast roll. Local control before remote control. Short duration before sustained operation. Each step earns the next one, rather than assuming the whole system works because the individual pieces tested fine in isolation.

06

Why Disciplined Documentation Matters

Every step of a well-run commissioning program produces a record: what was tested, under what conditions, by whom, with what result. That record is not paperwork for its own sake. It's the evidence that the machine is ready, it's the reference the next engineer uses when something changes years later, and it's the account that protects everyone (the engineer, the customer, and the equipment) if a question ever comes up about what was verified and when.

Commissioning is also inherently cross-functional. It requires coordination between mechanical, electrical, controls and safety disciplines, plus the people who will actually operate the equipment. Clear, accurate communication about status (especially while something is still in progress) is as much a part of the discipline as the technical verification itself.
