Engineering Profile

I'm a mechanical engineer specializing in the commissioning and combustion tuning of industrial gas turbines, working directly at operating power plants on machines already in service for Siemens Energy.

My career took a deliberate shape. Most engineering graduates begin one or two steps removed from the equipment they're responsible for. I chose field-based power generation engineering instead. An analysis I reach in the morning gets tested by the machine that same afternoon, in front of the people who own it, on a schedule that won't move.

The work spans commissioning, combustion tuning, controls and troubleshooting on large combustion turbines. It rewards precision, clear communication under pressure, and verifying instead of assuming.

Field Engineering & Commissioning

A combustion turbine is one of the most tightly constrained machines in industrial use. It converts fuel into hundreds of megawatts under conditions measured in milliseconds and single-digit parts per million. Every system around it has to agree with every other one before the unit is allowed to run: fuel, air, hydraulics, lube oil, water injection, instrumentation, controls.

Commissioning

I carry turbine and auxiliary systems from installation through verified function: calibration, fuel systems, water injection, lube and hydraulic oil, and instrumentation, each one proven against the control system, not taken on faith.

Combustion tuning

I tune low-load CO, optimize NOx and CO, and adjust fuel-to-air ratio and flame richness, analyzing combustion dynamics and pressure oscillations along the way. The goal is a stable operating window across the full load range.

Controls

I run functional loop checks, optimize control loops, and validate permissives and feedback. During startup, a mechanical symptom and a logic symptom can look identical, and separating them takes deliberate work.

Troubleshooting

I run root-cause analysis on startup failures and performance deviations, whether the fault sits in hardware, instrumentation, fuel, or logic. The sequence stays the same: reproduce, isolate, evidence, correct, verify, record.

Outage execution

I support major turbine outages through re-commissioning and return to service, coordinating with outage leadership, field service crews, controls specialists, and plant staff. Scope is fixed, sequence matters, and every hour is accounted for.

Customer engagement

I work daily with plant operators, customer engineers, and site leadership, reporting status accurately while a problem is still open. That reporting is part of the engineering: it determines whether the engineering gets used.

Engineering at Scale

Hundreds of
megawatts

A single combustion turbine can carry the electrical load of a mid-sized city, running against a grid that must hold sixty hertz within a few hundredths of a hertz, at every moment, without exception.

Milliseconds

Combustion dynamics are measured in frequency bands. A pressure oscillation that grows in phase with heat release can reach damaging amplitude in seconds, and the control system has to see it before the hardware does.

Parts per
million

Emissions compliance is written in single digits. The window that satisfies NOx, carbon monoxide, flame stability and efficiency simultaneously is narrow, and it moves with ambient temperature, fuel composition and machine condition.

Fixed windows

An outage schedule is built to the hour. Every day the unit is offline is generation the customer cannot sell. The work is performed around the clock, in the plant, with the people who own the consequence standing next to you.

Engineering Method

I work complicated problems with a small, well-ordered sequence that someone else can repeat and still reach the same answer. This is the loop I apply to a combustion turbine in the field.

  1. 01Observe
  2. 02Understand
  3. 03Diagnose
  4. 04Correct
  5. 05Measure
  6. 06Record
01

Observe

I collect what the system is actually doing before anyone explains what it should be doing.

02

Understand

I map the interactions and constraints. Most surprises live between subsystems, not inside them.

03

Diagnose

I isolate cause from symptom with evidence and name the failure mode precisely.

04

Correct

I design the correction: a change, a countermeasure, or a process that removes the cause.

05

Measure

I confirm results with data, since only a measured change counts as a real one.

06

Record

I document the work and hand it back in a state that holds without supervision.
